Why Cloud File Sharing Isn't Always the Best Option

The Upload-Then-Download Trap

When you share a file through Google Drive, iCloud or Dropbox, the file goes up to the provider's servers and then comes back down to the other device. That happens even when both devices sit on the same Wi-Fi network, a few feet apart.

For one photo, nobody notices. For a 4 GB video, you wait for two full transfers instead of one, and the first one is the slow one. Most home internet plans upload much more slowly than they download, so the upload sets the pace.

The math is simple. 4 GB is about 32,000 megabits. At 20 Mbps up, that is roughly 27 minutes before the other device can start downloading. At 100 Mbps up, it is still over 5 minutes.

A direct transfer on your local network skips the round trip. The file goes from one device to the other through your router and never crosses your internet connection, so your upload speed does not matter.

File Size Limits in Email and Cloud Sharing

Storage plans advertise total space. The limits that actually stop you are per file or per message.

  • Email attachments. Gmail caps attachments at 25 MB, and most other email services sit in the 20 to 25 MB range. Anything bigger gets turned into a cloud link anyway.
  • Cloud drives. Google Drive accepts single files up to 5 TB, so the cap is rarely the problem. The upload time is.
  • Transfer-link services. Free tiers cap the size of each transfer and expire the link after a while. The numbers change often, so check the current plan before you count on one.

When you hit a cap, the workarounds are all annoying: split the file, compress it, or pay for a bigger plan.

OpenDrop has no per-file size cap on either plan. On the same Wi-Fi it streams the whole file directly, with no chunking, even on Free. Away from home, Free sends the file in resumable 5 MB pieces and Pro streams it whole. In both cases a dropped connection picks up where it left off.

Tip

Keep a direct transfer tool installed next to your cloud storage. Use the cloud for share links and collaboration. Use direct transfer to move big files between your own devices.

The Privacy Cost of Free Cloud Storage

Every file you upload to a cloud service sits on hardware you don't control. That is the basic trade.

The big providers encrypt files in transit and at rest, but for standard storage they hold the keys. Apple, for example, holds the keys for most iCloud data unless you turn on Advanced Data Protection. Providers also scan stored files for malware and for content that breaks their terms.

For vacation photos, that is fine. For tax returns, medical records, contracts or unreleased work, routing them through a third party leaves a copy somewhere you don't need one.

A transfer on your local network stays inside it. The data goes from one device to the other through your router and never reaches the public internet. You don't have to turn anything on for that. It is how local networking works.

Once your devices are on different networks, something has to carry the data. OpenDrop uses a direct peer-to-peer link or its own encrypted relay. File contents are encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305 on your device before they leave it. The relay carries them only in encrypted form, but it can see file names, file sizes and device names, which it needs to route the transfer. The encryption guide covers this in more detail.

Speed: Cloud Round-Trip vs Direct Transfer

A cloud transfer is bound by your internet upload speed. A local transfer is bound by your Wi-Fi or wired network. On most home setups the local network is the faster of the two.

Here is where the time goes when you share through the cloud between two devices in the same room:

  1. Upload the whole file at your upload speed.
  2. Wait for the service to finish processing it and for the other device to notice it. Sync clients can add a delay here.
  3. Download the whole file at your download speed.

A direct LAN transfer is one step: send the file at local network speed. A good Wi-Fi 5 or Wi-Fi 6 link often moves a few hundred megabits per second in practice, and wired gigabit Ethernet does better. That is usually several times a typical home upload speed.

Remote transfers are a closer contest. When the devices are on different networks, the cloud and a direct tool both depend on your internet connection. OpenDrop Free sends remote transfers over a direct peer-to-peer link or the shared free relay. They are speed-limited and share a weekly fair-use allowance. Pro uses a faster relay with no weekly limit. Transfers on the same Wi-Fi are never limited on either plan. See sending large files from phone to PC for the details.

When Cloud Storage Makes Sense

Cloud storage is the right tool for several jobs, and direct transfer does not replace it for these.

Sharing with other people. To send a file to someone who has no special app installed, maybe on another continent, a share link is hard to beat. You can't expect everyone to install a transfer app.

Access from every device. Sync keeps your files on every device, all the time. Edit a document on your phone at lunch and it is on your desktop that evening. A direct transfer is a one-time copy, not ongoing sync.

Collaboration. Real-time co-editing in Google Docs and similar tools needs a shared server. Direct transfer doesn't do that.

Off-site backup. If your house floods, the drive in the closet is gone and the cloud copy survives. Transfer tools move files. They are not a backup.

Setting Up Direct Transfer as Your Default

Use both. Cloud for sharing, sync and backup. Direct transfer for moving files between your own devices.

Step 1: Install a Direct Transfer Tool

OpenDrop runs on Windows, Linux, iPhone, iPad and Android. macOS is coming soon. Install it on your computer and your phone, then scan the QR code on the computer with the phone. Free needs no account. On the same Wi-Fi, the phone then finds the computer over mDNS and streams files directly at full speed.

On Free, each phone pairs with one computer. Pro connects every phone, tablet and computer signed in to your account, and adds phone to phone and computer to computer transfers. The Windows setup guide walks through the install.

Step 2: Change Your Habit for Device-to-Device Transfers

Next time you need a file from your phone on your PC, send it directly instead of uploading it somewhere. Received files land in %USERPROFILE%\Downloads\OpenDrop\Files on Windows and ~/Downloads/OpenDrop/Files on Linux.

Step 3: Keep Cloud for What It Does Best

Keep the cloud for files that need to be reachable from anywhere, shared with other people, or backed up off-site. Synced folders, shared documents and photo backup belong there. A video you are moving from your phone to your desktop for editing does not.

Step 4: Test the Speed Difference

Pick a file over 500 MB and time both methods. For the cloud, measure from the start of the upload to the end of the download. Then time a direct transfer on the same Wi-Fi. Your own numbers are the ones that matter, since they depend on your internet plan and your router.
