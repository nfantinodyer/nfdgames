How to Transfer Photos from iPhone to Windows PC

A Quick Decision Framework

No single method wins. Three questions decide it: how many photos you're moving, whether you need the original files, and whether this is a one-off or something you do every week.

  1. A few photos, right now: email or a messaging app. Nothing to set up, but expect compression.
  2. A big batch at full quality: a USB cable or a direct Wi-Fi transfer app. You get the original files with no cloud in between.
  3. Automatic sync in the background: iCloud for Windows. It needs an Apple Account and, for most libraries, paid iCloud storage.
  4. Regular transfers without a cable: a Wi-Fi transfer app such as OpenDrop, which pairs your iPhone with your PC.

USB Cable: The Reliable Default

Connect the iPhone to the PC with a cable. Unlock the phone and tap Trust when it asks about this computer. Then use one of these:

  • Windows Photos app: choose Import, pick the iPhone, and select the photos from a thumbnail grid.
  • File Explorer: open This PC, then Apple iPhone, Internal Storage, DCIM, and copy the folders you want.

No Wi-Fi, no account, no app on the phone. For thousands of photos at once, the cable is still the simplest and fastest route.

Check the cable first. iPhone 15 and later use USB-C. Older models use Lightning. The other end needs to match your PC's port, USB-A or USB-C.

The DCIM folder is awkward to browse. Photos sit in subfolders such as 100APPLE, with names like IMG_0001.HEIC. Your albums don't exist here, so finding one specific photo means scrolling through thumbnails.

Then there's HEIC. iPhones save photos as HEIC by default because the files are much smaller than JPEG at similar quality. To open HEIC photos, Windows 10 and 11 need two Microsoft Store add-ons: the free HEIF Image Extensions and the HEVC Video Extensions, which many PCs already have preinstalled. The HEVC add-on also plays iPhone videos. Some older photo editors can't open either format.

Two iPhone settings control what you get:

  • Settings, Photos, Transfer to Mac or PC. Automatic converts photos to JPEG as they copy over USB. Keep Originals copies the HEIC files unchanged. If a large copy stops partway with a "device is unreachable" error, switching to Keep Originals usually fixes it.
  • Settings, Camera, Formats, Most Compatible. New photos are saved as JPEG from then on. They take more space on the phone.

Best for: large one-time transfers where you want the original files.

iCloud for Windows: When You Already Pay for Storage

Apple's iCloud for Windows app syncs your iCloud Photos library to the PC. Install it from the Microsoft Store, sign in with your Apple Account, and turn on Photos. From then on, new photos from the iPhone show up on the PC without any manual steps.

What it costs you:

  • Storage. Free iCloud storage is 5 GB, and a phone camera fills that quickly. Past that you need a paid iCloud+ plan, billed monthly.
  • Time. Every photo uploads to Apple's servers and then downloads to the PC. The first sync of a large library can take hours, depending on your internet connection.
  • Privacy. Your whole library is stored on Apple's servers. With standard data protection, Apple holds the encryption keys for iCloud Photos. Advanced Data Protection changes that if you turn it on.

Best for: people who already pay for iCloud storage and want photos to appear on the PC automatically.

Email and Messaging Apps: The Small-Batch Shortcut

Select a few photos in the Photos app, tap Share, and send them by email or a messaging app. Open the message on the PC and save the attachments.

For one to five photos this is the quickest option, because the apps are already on both devices. The limits:

  • Email attachments are capped. Gmail allows 25 MB per message, and iPhone Mail may offer to send smaller versions of the images.
  • Many messaging apps compress photos by default, which lowers resolution and quality. Some have an option to send the original file or send it as a document.
  • iMessage has no Windows app.

Compression doesn't matter for a quick look. It does matter for printing, editing or archiving.

Best for: a handful of photos where original quality doesn't matter.

Direct Wi-Fi Transfer: No Cable, No Cloud

A transfer app sends photos from the phone straight to the PC over your local network. There's no cable, no cloud storage, and no compression.

Here's how OpenDrop does it. The Windows app runs a small local server (port 8000 by default) and announces itself on the network with mDNS (_opendrop._tcp.local.). After you pair the iPhone by scanning a QR code, the phone finds the PC on the same Wi-Fi and streams files to it directly at full speed. Received files land in %USERPROFILE%\Downloads\OpenDrop\Files by default.

  1. Install OpenDrop on the PC and on the iPhone.
  2. Open the app on the PC. It shows a QR code.
  3. Scan the code with the OpenDrop app on the iPhone. That pairs the two. Free needs no account.
  4. Pick photos on the phone and send them. Keep the app open until a Wi-Fi transfer finishes.

Photos arrive as the original HEIC files at full quality. If you need JPEGs, OpenDrop Pro can convert HEIC to JPG on the PC, on demand or with an automatic rule you turn on. Conversion always runs on the computer, and the original is backed up there so you can undo it.

Tip

You don't need to be on the same Wi-Fi. With the iPhone on cellular and the PC at home, OpenDrop picks a direct peer-to-peer link or its own encrypted relay. There's nothing extra to install and no port forwarding. On Free, remote transfers are speed-limited, move in resumable 5 MB pieces, and share a weekly allowance. Pro uses a faster relay with no weekly limit. The relay carries photo contents only in encrypted form, though it can see file names and sizes. Transfers on the same Wi-Fi are never limited.

Compared to USB, Wi-Fi is usually slower for a huge dump, but you skip the cable and the DCIM folders. Compared to iCloud, nothing goes through Apple's servers and you don't need a storage plan. The tradeoff is that transfers are manual: you choose what to send each time.

Best for: regular transfers at original quality, without a cable or a cloud account.

Which Method to Pick

Setup before the first transfer (least to most):

  1. Email or messaging: nothing to set up.
  2. USB cable: plug in, trust the PC, copy.
  3. Wi-Fi transfer app: install on both devices, scan a QR code.
  4. iCloud for Windows: install, sign in, wait for the first sync.

Effort for repeat transfers (least to most):

  1. iCloud: automatic.
  2. Wi-Fi transfer app: open the app on both devices, select, send.
  3. USB cable: find the cable, plug in, browse, copy.
  4. Email or messaging: share, open on the PC, save each attachment.

Photo quality:

  • USB cable: originals, or JPEG copies if Transfer to Mac or PC is set to Automatic.
  • Wi-Fi transfer app: originals.
  • iCloud: originals, after a round trip through Apple's servers.
  • Email or messaging: often compressed.

Without internet:

  • USB cable: works.
  • Wi-Fi transfer app: works when both devices are on the same local network.
  • Email, messaging and iCloud: need internet.

A simple rule: if you do this once a year, use the cable. If you do it every week, set up a Wi-Fi transfer app. If you want it fully automatic and don't mind paying for storage, use iCloud. For thousands of photos at once, plug in the cable regardless.
